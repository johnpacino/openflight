Watching a golf ball at 60 GHz

We replaced the K-LD7 angle radars with a single $196 TI IWR6843 running our own firmware — and captured full golf shots in raw radar data for the first time. This is the approach, what the first sessions showed, and where the hard limits are.

Sensor TI IWR6843 (60–64 GHz FMCW) Firmware custom, variant B First shots July 12–13, 2026 Status speed + launch angle live · TrackMan validation next

Recap

Where the K-LD7 left us

The previous rig measured launch angle with two K-LD7 modules — small 24 GHz radars that report the phase difference between two receive antennas, which converts to an angle. After a lot of work (trajectory fitting, multipath handling, per-club tiers) it reached roughly ±2° on launch angle. But it had three ceilings we could never raise:

  • Starved for data. Indoors, the ball flies for ~50 ms before the net stops it. Against the K-LD7's fixed 29 ms frame cadence, that meant one or two frames of ball flight per swing — every launch angle balanced on a measurement or two.
  • Two antennas, one angle. Phase interferometry with a 2-element array has no way to tell a clean echo from a corrupted one. When the floor bounced a second copy of the ball's echo into the receiver, the angle simply bent — and near the ground, it always did.
  • Not enough bandwidth — even with raw data. We did crack the module open: streamed its raw ADC and ran our own detection and angle code on it. It still wasn't enough, because the ceiling is regulatory physics: at 24 GHz the legal band is ~250 MHz wide, which makes each range pixel ~60 cm. The ball, its floor reflection, and half the golfer occupy one pixel — no algorithm can separate what the radio never resolved. That lesson generalizes to any 24 GHz front end, however many channels it has.

Instrument

The new sensor, and the decision that matters

The IWR6843 is an automotive-grade radar-on-a-chip. It transmits chirps — rapid frequency sweeps — and the delay of each returning echo encodes distance. Sweeping 3.2 GHz of bandwidth slices the world into 4.7 cm range bins: for the first time, we see where the ball is, not just how fast it moves. And with 2 transmit × 4 receive antennas it forms an 8-element virtual array — enough aperture to measure angles properly, and to eventually tell a real echo from its floor reflection.

The decision that matters: we don't use the chip's built-in processing at all. Our firmware continuously records the raw antenna signal into a rolling buffer of on-chip memory, and the same sound trigger that served the old rig freezes it at impact. Everything downstream — clutter removal, tracking, angle estimation — runs in Python on the Raspberry Pi, where we can see, question, and improve every step. The K-LD7's black box is gone.

The unlock

A rolling buffer in the chip's own memory

Raw radar data normally has a toll gate: the chip's ADCs produce ~10 MB/s, so vendors sell capture cards (TI's is ~$755, plus LVDS cabling and a PC in the loop) to siphon it out. It's why hobby radar projects settle for a chip's processed output.

The move that makes this project work: we never try to stream it all. The IWR6843 carries 768 KB of on-chip "radar cube" memory, and our firmware wires the receive hardware to DMA every chirp straight into a ring there — the chip is always holding the last ~72 ms of raw, coherent, 8-virtual-channel data, with zero CPU involvement and zero external hardware. The impact sound freezes the ring; a plain UART drains those 786 KB to the Raspberry Pi in 7.6 s. A golf shot is over in 50 ms — you don't need continuous capture, you need the right 72 ms, and the trigger already knows when that is.

This is what raw data buys over any processed point-cloud: every antenna's complex signal, every chirp, reprocessable forever. A mount-tilt error, a 40° antenna-feed phase offset, and a 50 ms trigger bug were each found after the fact, in saved shot data. On a processed-output radar, all three would still be invisible.

Capture

How a shot is recorded

The radar chirps in short bursts — 64 chirps in 2.9 ms, every 12 ms — writing each burst into a six-slot ring that always holds the last 72 ms. The impact sound trips the trigger; the firmware waits 50 ms so the ring fills with ball flight, then freezes and streams all 786 KB to the Pi. The window ends up holding a little of the downswing, the strike, and the entire flight to the net.

Findings

The ball is in the data — every swing

A golf ball is a tiny target sharing the scene with a moving human fifty times brighter. Raw range profiles never show it; the essential step is clutter removal — subtracting everything stationary — after which the ball appears as a faint diagonal streak marching outward, frame by frame:

Ball speed comes from that streak — how fast the ball's distance changes — and the tracks are clean enough that the fits repeat to a fraction of a range bin. A session of 9-irons read 97–105 mph with believable shot-to-shot variation.

Why not just use Doppler? Radar's classic speed trick fails here, and it's worth knowing why: with our chirp timing, any speed above 30 mph aliases — like a wagon wheel spinning backward on film. A 110 mph ball reports as walking pace. Distance-over-time is immune to this, which is exactly why we captured raw data with real range resolution in the first place.

Launch angle is the harder prize, and the early results are genuinely promising. Each chirp burst gives dozens of full 8-antenna snapshots; comparing phases across the array yields the ball's elevation angle, and stringing those together traces the launch in the vertical plane — dozens of angle measurements per shot where the K-LD7 gave one or two. Three estimators fit that trajectory side by side (an unconstrained line, a line anchored at the tape-measured tee, and a physics model of the floor bounce), and their per-shot agreement is itself a quality signal.

Two results gave us confidence the physics chain is honest. First, every shot's trajectory, extrapolated back to the tee, insisted the radar was tilted up by ~10.4° — and when we checked the mount, it was. The data discovered its own installation error. Second, a ten-minute calibration with a corner reflector (a metal target whose position we tape-measured) pinned the remaining systematics: ranges read 6.6 cm long, a constant; and one antenna group carried a fixed 40° phase offset that had been quietly bending every angle until that day.

Experiment

Five firmwares, forty swings, one memory budget

The chip's capture memory is a hard 768 KB, and everything trades inside it: window length × frame count × bytes per frame. Rather than argue theory, we built five allocations and hit eight 9-irons against each in a single day, every dump self-labeling its variant.

Three lessons. Frames are the currency of launch-angle quality — going from 6 looks at the flight to 12 (A→B) tightened angle consistency 3×, even though each look got thinner. But only clean frames count — D2's 24 coarse-binned frames lost to B's 12 fine ones; past a dozen frames, per-snapshot signal quality dominates. And fine range bins matter beyond speed: they separate ball from body and club from hands, and they feed the floor-bounce model that coarse bins starve.

Variant B is now production (16 chirp pairs × 12 frames × 6 ms, full-resolution bins). D1 stays in the quiver for fast-cadence range sessions — same rhythm, coarser bins, dumps in 3.7 s instead of 7.6 for ~1° of consistency. And the path forward is drawn: on-chip pre-processing that keeps only the range bins that matter would fit 24 full-resolution frames in half the memory — B's quality, twice the frames, ~3-second dumps.

Blind spots

Where this radar can't see — and what we do about it

  • solvedDoppler lies about fast balls. The textbook radar speed trick aliases above ~30 mph with our chirp timing — a 110 mph ball reports as walking pace. We simply don't use it: with 4.7 cm range pixels, speed comes from the ball's position change across the window — unambiguous at any speed, and repeatable to ~1 cm on real shots. This is a solved problem, not a compromise: it's the speed source that makes single-chip ball speed viable.
  • by designThe radar blinks. It chirps ~1.4 ms out of every 6, so a shot is sampled in 12 bursts rather than continuously. With ~80–100 usable measurements per shot, the blinks haven't cost a single ball this weekend.
  • configNear field and the net. Below ~0.35 m the transmitter's own leakage masks returns, and past the net everything is fabric physics. Both edges are now measured configuration — tape the tee, tape the net, and the pipeline fits only the clean flight between them (a ball riding up the net is an upward mover that would otherwise tilt angles high).
  • physicsThe floor bounce is still the enemy — the same one that beat the K-LD7. The difference is the arsenal: at 60 GHz the bounce is often separable in range (impossible at 24 GHz), the 8-element array can tell two arrival angles apart, per-point quality gates reject corrupted snapshots, and the two-ray estimator models the reflection as a second, geometry-locked signal — using it instead of suffering it. Residual bias is measurable and correctable against ground truth.
  • throughput~8 s blind after each shot while the ring drains (down from 17). Roadmap: on-chip pre-processing keeps only the range pixels that matter — ~3 s dumps and twice the frames, no new hardware.
  • unprovenAbsolute accuracy awaits ground truth. Precision is measured (~±2° per-shot fit; estimators consistent across sessions); the systematic offsets between estimators (3–4°) are exactly what a TrackMan side-by-side turns into correction constants — the same play that calibrated the K-LD7, with far better raw material.
